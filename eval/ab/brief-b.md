# Brief B: Should we move the company to a 4-day work week?

## 1. The question, restated

In economic terms this is a question about the **hours–productivity gradient** (does the marginal hour produce as much as the average hour?), **compensating differentials** (is a shorter week a form of compensation workers will trade wages — or quits — for?), and **selection** (do the firms and workers who try this differ from those who don't?). The relevant subfields are labor economics (hours and productivity), personnel economics (firm-level work design), and the experimental literature on flexible work. A subtle but important distinction runs through everything below: a **4-day week with fewer total hours** (the 100:80:100 model — 100% pay, 80% time, 100% output) is a different intervention from a **compressed week** (4×10), and most of the famous "4-day week" evidence is actually about modest hours reductions, not four-day weeks at all.

## 2. What the canon says

- **Pencavel (2015):** Output is not proportional to hours — beyond a threshold, each additional hour yields diminishing returns, because fatigue accumulates. Cutting hours from a long baseline costs less output than a linear model predicts.
- **Mas & Pallais (2017):** Workers will pay real money for better work arrangements (8% of wages for work-from-home; 20% to avoid employer-controlled short-notice scheduling). A shorter week is compensation, and it should buy you hiring and retention.
- **Bloom, Liang, Roberts & Ying (2015):** The closest thing to a credible firm-level RCT on flexible work: +13% performance, halved attrition — but among *volunteers*, with a promotion penalty and big selection effects when choice was introduced.

## 3. What's new

- **Iceland trials (2015–19):** 2,500 workers, hours cut with no pay loss, productivity held or improved — but it was a 1–4 hour reduction, not a 4-day week, and the permanent deals that followed delivered under an hour a week.
- **UK pilot (2022):** 61 volunteer firms, ~2,900 workers, 6 months of 100:80:100: large wellbeing gains, revenue stable-to-up, quits down sharply — but no control group, volunteer-selected firms, and a 6-month horizon.
- **Delaney & Casey (2022):** the critical voice — qualitative evidence from New Zealand that compressing the same workload into fewer days intensified work and raised stress for some employees.

## 4. Paper briefs

> **Pencavel, John H. (2015), "The Productivity of Working Hours," *Economic Journal* 125(589), 2052–2076.**
> - Question: what is the shape of the output–hours relationship — is an hour an hour?
> - Identification: observational production-function estimation on British WWI munitions workers (mostly women). Not experimental: hours varied across workers and weeks for reasons the paper must assume are unrelated to unobserved worker quality. Moderately credible for the *shape* of the relationship; weaker for exact magnitudes.
> - Magnitude: nonlinear — below a threshold, output is roughly proportional to hours; above it, output rises at a decreasing rate. (I could not verify the exact threshold or elasticities from the abstract; the paper's contribution is the functional form, not a single number.)
> - Setting: wartime munitions factories, 1910s Britain. Far from a modern office — but the mechanism (fatigue, insufficient recovery) is physiological, which is why economists still cite it.
> - Mechanism: long weeks cause fatigue and crowd out recovery time, so subsequent performance suffers (developed further in his 2016 follow-up on recovery).
> - Critique: selection into long hours is not random; wartime effort norms may not transfer; says nothing about *which* hours to cut (the fifth day vs. shorter days).
> - Status: well-established classic in labor economics; the standard citation for diminishing returns to working time.

> **Mas, Alexandre & Pallais, Amanda (2017), "Valuing Alternative Work Arrangements," *American Economic Review* 107(12), 3722–3759.**
> - Question: how much do workers value non-wage aspects of jobs — schedule control, work-from-home?
> - Identification: field experiment embedded in real hiring for a national call center. Applicants were randomly offered traditional vs. alternative arrangements with randomly varied wage gaps, tracing out the willingness-to-pay distribution. Credible randomization; the authors document and adjust for applicant inattention.
> - Magnitude: the average applicant would give up **20% of wages to avoid an employer-set short-notice schedule** and **8% for the option to work from home**. Most workers won't pay for flexible scheduling per se — but a high-WTP tail supports sizable compensating differentials. Women, especially with young children, value these amenities most.
> - Setting: US call-center job applicants. Knowledge workers likely value schedule amenities at least as much, but the exact percentages shouldn't be ported.
> - Mechanism: compensating differentials — amenities substitute for wages in the total compensation bundle, letting firms hire and retain at lower pay (or, equivalently, cut quits without raising pay).
> - Critique: WTP is estimated at the hiring margin for one occupation; it measures *aversion to bad schedules* more than *demand for fewer days*; and the experiment predates the post-Covid normalization of flexibility, which may have shifted valuations.
> - Status: modern classic; the benchmark paper on what flexibility is worth to workers.

> **Bloom, Nicholas, Liang, James, Roberts, John & Ying, Zhichun Jenny (2015), "Does Working from Home Work? Evidence from a Chinese Experiment," *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 130(1), 165–218.**
> - Question: does working from home raise or lower performance?
> - Identification: RCT — Ctrip call-center employees who volunteered for WFH were randomized by even/odd birthday into home vs. office for 9 months. Clean randomization, balance checked, and a second call center used to rule out spillovers onto the control group. The estimand is the effect **among volunteers**, not among all employees.
> - Magnitude: **+13% performance** for home workers — ~9pp from more minutes worked per shift (fewer breaks/sick days), ~4pp from more calls per minute (quieter environment) — off a standard call-center baseline. **Attrition halved.** But **promotion rates conditional on performance fell ~50%**, and the firm estimated savings of ~$2,000/employee/year. When workers could later choose freely, gains nearly **doubled to 22%** as people sorted into the arrangement that suited them.
> - Setting: Chinese travel-agency call centers, 2010–11. Individual, metered output; no team production.
> - Mechanism: fewer interruptions + no commute on marginal-sick days → more effective minutes; but physical absence → lower visibility → fewer promotions.
> - Critique: volunteer selection is the whole ballgame — the 13% is not the effect of forcing everyone home, and the 22% post-choice figure shows selection on gains. Call centers ≠ collaborative knowledge work; one firm, one country.
> - Status: canon for firm-level work-design experiments; the paper every remote-work debate cites.

> **Haraldsson, Guðmundur D. & Kellam, Jack (2021), *Going Public: Iceland's Journey to a Shorter Working Week*, Alda & Autonomy (think-tank report, June 2021).**
> - Question: can hours be cut with no pay loss without hurting public-sector output?
> - Identification: **none, causally.** Two large trials (Reykjavík City Council, then national government), 66 workplaces, ~2,500 workers, 2015–19, rolled out in waves with before/after comparisons. No randomization, no control group. This is pre-post evidence with all the usual confounds.
> - Magnitude: hours fell from 40 to 35–36/week (a **max 4-hour cut; in 61 of 66 workplaces just 1–3 hours**) with no pay reduction. Productivity and service provision "remained the same or improved across the majority of trial workplaces"; stress and burnout fell. The honest scale check: the permanent union agreements that followed delivered **~35 min/week (private sector) and ~65 min/week (public sector)** — not a four-day week.
> - Setting: Icelandic public sector — offices, preschools, hospitals, social services, 2015–19.
> - Mechanism: workplaces cut low-value time — fewer/shorter meetings, shorter breaks, services moved online. The gain is reorganization, not rest per se.
> - Critique: the "4-day week" framing is media spin the report itself barely uses (two mentions in 80+ pages). Productivity is largely manager/employee-reported, not metered. And the publisher (Alda) had advocated shorter hours since 2011 — advocacy research with no pre-registration deserves discounting, not dismissal.
> - Status: **influential report, not peer-reviewed science.** Treat as suggestive field evidence, never as consensus.

> **Autonomy et al. (2023), *The Results Are In: The UK's Four-Day Week Pilot* (Autonomy; quantitative team led by Juliet Schor, Boston College; qualitative team incl. David Frayne & Brendan Burchell, Cambridge), February 2023 (report).**
> - Question: does the 100:80:100 model (100% pay, 80% time, 100% output) work across industries?
> - Identification: **pre-post with no control group.** 61 *self-selected* firms, ~2,900 workers, June–Dec 2022, with employee surveys at three waves and company admin data. No randomization; 2022's post-Covid reopening boom is an obvious confound for revenue comparisons.
> - Magnitude: 39% of employees less stressed, 71% lower burnout — but **13% more stressed and 22% higher burnout** (real heterogeneity, not a clean win). Revenue **+1.4% on average** across the 23 firms reporting it (+35% vs. the same period in prior years — a comparison that mostly reflects 2022 vs. 2021, not the intervention). **Sick days fell ~2/3; quits fell 57%** vs. the prior year. 56 of 61 firms continued; 18 made it permanent. A year later, 54 still had the policy.
> - Setting: UK firms that volunteered, spanning nonprofits to manufacturers to a fish-and-chip shop, 2022.
> - Mechanism: same as Iceland — work intensification via cutting meetings and low-value time — plus a retention channel (the 57% quit drop is the firm's ROI).
> - Critique: volunteer firms are the ones that expected it to work (Bloom's selection lesson, writ large). No control group means the revenue and quit numbers can't be causally attributed. Six months is too short to see promotion, innovation, or burnout-from-intensification effects. Wellbeing is self-reported during a heavily publicized trial (Hawthorne risk).
> - Status: the most-cited 4-day-week evidence base — and still a **non-experimental advocacy-adjacent report**. Encouraging, not dispositive.

> **Delaney, Helen & Casey, Catherine (2022), "The promise of a four-day week? A critical appraisal of a management-led initiative," *Employee Relations* 44(1), 176–190.**
> - Question: what happens inside a firm that adopts a 4-day week — for everyone, including the stressed?
> - Identification: qualitative case study — interviews/focus groups with ~45 employees at Perpetual Guardian (NZ trust company), the 2018 pioneer. Descriptive by design; no causal claims possible, but rich on mechanism.
> - Magnitude: no numbers — the finding is directional: compressing the same workload raised work *intensity*, and a minority experienced more stress, not less.
> - Setting: one New Zealand financial-services firm, 2018.
> - Mechanism: **work intensification** — output held because pace rose, and pace is a cost borne unevenly (customer-facing and junior staff felt it most).
> - Critique: single firm, small N, qualitative — it can't tell you how common the downside is, only that it exists. But as a check on the trial literature's sunny averages, that's exactly its value.
> - Status: peer-reviewed but niche; useful as the steelman of the skeptical case, not as an effect size.

## 5. What economists would argue about

- **Selection is the first-order problem.** Every encouraging number comes from volunteers — firms that opted into trials, workers who wanted fewer hours. Bloom et al. show the volunteer estimand differs from the everyone estimand *and* that selection can cut either way (their gains doubled with choice). The UK and Iceland trials cannot tell you what happens at a firm that adopts this reluctantly or firm-wide by mandate.
- **Hours vs. days, and how much.** Pencavel's diminishing-returns logic plus Iceland's actual 1–4 hour cuts suggest the active ingredient is *fewer total hours from a long baseline*, not the label "four-day." A 4×10 compressed week is a different treatment with different fatigue implications — and Delaney & Casey show compression can intensify rather than relieve.
- **Who pays the 25% hourly raise.** 100:80:100 is a 25% increase in the hourly wage. In a competitive labor market that's a compensating differential (Mas & Pallais): it should reduce quits and hiring costs — the UK's 57% quit drop is consistent — but if *every* firm adopts it, the retention advantage washes out and you're left with the cost. This is the general-equilibrium caveat the trial literature never addresses.
- **Measurement and horizon.** "Productivity maintained" in the trials is mostly self- and manager-reported over 6 months. Bloom's RCT, with metered output, found a promotion penalty invisible at 6 months and selection effects that only appeared after choice was introduced. The long-run career and innovation effects of a 4-day week are unmeasured everywhere.
- **Coordination costs.** A firm on 4 days interacting with clients, vendors, and teams on 5 faces a coordination tax. None of the trials randomize at the level where this binds, and it's plausibly the largest cost for B2B and customer-facing roles.

## 6. Bottom line for your question

**The literature supports running a serious pilot, not a blind rollout.** The honest read across these six sources:

1. If your baseline is long hours and output is individually measurable, cutting hours will cost less output than a linear projection says (Pencavel), and the freed-up compensation value should show up in retention (Mas & Pallais; the UK quit data).
2. The headline trial results are real but inflated by volunteer selection and the absence of control groups — discount them accordingly, and note that "Iceland's 4-day week" was a 1–4 hour cut whose permanent legacy was under an hour a week.
3. Expect the gains to come from *work redesign* (killing low-value meetings and busywork), not from rest alone — and watch for intensification hurting a minority (Delaney & Casey).
4. The promotion-visibility cost in Bloom is the underappreciated risk for knowledge workers: less face time can mean slower advancement, which compounds over years.

**What would change the answer:** (a) your baseline hours — if people already work ~35 effective hours, there's no fat to cut and Pencavel's logic stops helping; (b) how much of your work is team-produced or client-facing on a 5-day rhythm (coordination costs, and SUTVA-style interference, argue for team-level rather than individual pilots); (c) a within-firm randomized pilot — randomize *teams* to 4-day vs. 5-day for 6–12 months with pre-registered, metered productivity metrics and quit rates, which would give you a credible ATT for your own firm instead of borrowing one from Icelandic preschools.

## 7. Open threads

- **4×8 vs. 4×10 vs. 5×7:** the trials mostly tested genuine hours *reduction*; the compressed-week variant is a different treatment with almost no credible evidence.
- **Long-run effects on promotion, innovation, and skill formation** — unmeasured in every trial; Bloom's promotion finding is the warning light.
- **Heterogeneity by role:** who gains (deep-work roles?) vs. who pays the intensification cost (client-facing, junior staff?) — the UK data hints at it but doesn't nail it.
- **General equilibrium:** what happens to the retention benefit — and to wages — if the 4-day week becomes the market norm rather than a differentiator.
- **Optimal design of your own pilot:** team-level randomization, pre-registered outcomes, and a long enough horizon to see past the Hawthorne window.
