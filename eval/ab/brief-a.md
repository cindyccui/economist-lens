# Brief A: Should we move the company to a 4-day work week?

**Bottom line:** The evidence is genuinely encouraging — trials consistently show maintained output, lower burnout, and sharply lower attrition — but every positive study suffers from volunteer-company selection and mostly self-reported outcomes. Don't leap; run a 6-month internal pilot with a control group and pre-specified metrics. Details below.

## What the evidence shows

**1. The largest cross-country trial finds well-being gains with no productivity collapse.**
Fan, Schor, Kelly & Gu (2025), "Work time reduction via a four-day week finds improvements in workers' well-being," *Nature Human Behaviour*, tracked 2,896 employees across 141 companies in six countries (US, UK, Canada, Australia, Ireland, NZ) over six months, against a control group of ~300 employees at 12 companies that kept five-day weeks. Nearly 70% of employees reported less burnout, over 40% reported improved mental health, and 38% reported better sleep. Gains were dose-dependent — workers cutting 8+ hours saw the largest improvements — and persisted at a 12-month follow-up. Over 90% of companies kept the schedule.

**2. The UK pilot: revenue held, attrition fell.**
The 2022 UK trial (61 companies, ~2,900 employees, June–December 2022; research led by Juliet Schor and Wen Fan of Boston College with David Frayne and Brendan Burchell of Cambridge, reported via Autonomy in 2023) found revenue rose 1.4% on average during the trial, sick days fell 65% (2.0 → 0.7 days/employee/month), and resignations fell 57% versus the prior year. 92% of companies continued afterward; 18 made it permanent.

**3. Iceland: public-sector proof of concept at scale.**
Haraldsson & Kellam (2021), "Going Public: Iceland's journey to a shorter working week" (Autonomy/Alda), analyzed two trials covering 2,500 public-sector workers (2015–2019), cutting 40 → 35–36 hours with no pay cut. Productivity and service provision stayed the same or improved in most workplaces; well-being rose across stress, burnout, and work-life balance measures. 86% of Iceland's workforce has since moved to shorter hours. Caveat: public-sector workplaces with strong baseline conditions.

**4. The productivity mechanism is real: marginal hours are the least productive.**
Pencavel (2015), "The Productivity of Working Hours," *Economic Journal* 125(589), using WWI British munitions-worker data, finds output is proportional to hours only below a threshold; above it, each additional hour yields diminishing output. Collewet & Sauermann (2017), "Working hours and productivity," *Labour Economics* 47, using day-level call-center data with within-worker variation from central scheduling, find longer hours increase average call-handling time — i.e., fatigue directly lowers per-hour productivity, even among part-timers. This is *why* output can hold when hours fall: you're cutting the least productive hours.

**5. The cautionary case: hours cuts with full pay raise hourly labor costs.**
Crépon & Kramarz (2002), "Employed 40 Hours or Not Employed 39," *Journal of Political Economy* 110(6), studied France's 1982 mandatory cut from 40 → 39 hours with monthly earnings held constant. Result: 2–4% job losses, concentrated among minimum-wage workers. The mechanism matters — when the hourly cost of labor rises and firms can't adjust, employment falls. A 4-day week at full pay is a ~25% increase in the hourly wage; the trials above show firms absorbing it through productivity and retention, but it's a cost, not free.

## Honest caveats

- **Selection.** Every trial consists of companies that volunteered — likely those already expecting it to work. The Fan et al. control group is companies that expressed interest but didn't participate, not random assignment. True effects for the average firm are probably smaller.
- **Measurement.** Well-being outcomes are self-reported; employees who want to keep the extra day off have reason to report positively. Firm-level productivity is rarely measured with hard output data (the UK revenue figures come from 23 of 61 companies).
- **Heterogeneity.** Effects concentrate in knowledge/service work where output isn't strictly tied to hours present. Customer-facing, shift-based, or coverage-critical roles (support, ops, retail) face a real staffing-arithmetic problem: someone still has to cover Friday.

## Recommendation

**Run a 6-month pilot, not a company-wide switch.** Design it to beat the existing evidence:

1. **Stagger rollout across teams** to create an internal control group; pre-commit to metrics: output per team (your real KPIs, not self-reports), sick days, regretted attrition, hiring cost/time-to-fill, and customer SLA adherence.
2. **Price the cost explicitly:** model the ~25% hourly wage increase against expected savings from attrition (replacing an employee typically costs 50–200% of annual salary) and absenteeism. The UK trial's 57% drop in resignations is where the ROI likely lives, if it replicates.
3. **Solve coverage first:** staggered days off, not everyone out Friday, for any customer-facing function.
4. **Decision rule in advance:** e.g., continue only if team output is within 5% of baseline and attrition/absenteeism improve. The 90%+ continuation rate in trials partly reflects sunk enthusiasm — your bar should be numerical.

The literature says this is a reasonable bet with real upside on retention and well-being, real downside risk on labor cost, and no credible evidence of an output collapse in comparable settings. A well-measured pilot turns an open question into your own data.
